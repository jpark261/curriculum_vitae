Honors Thesis

2026

The Uneven Growth Cost of Political Instability

Background

The thesis revisits whether political instability slows economic growth and asks for whom the effect is largest. It builds on Aisen and Veiga (2013), but argues that pooled cross-country estimates can hide large differences between fragile low-income economies and institutionally consolidated high-income economies.

Data

The analysis uses a global panel of 185 countries over 1960-2023, organized into non-overlapping five-year periods. Growth and macroeconomic controls are updated using Penn World Table 11.0, with political instability measured primarily through cabinet changes.

Methodology

The thesis first performs a near-reproduction of Aisen and Veiga's dynamic panel growth regressions, then extends the sample and estimates heterogeneous effects by income group. The estimator comparison includes pooled OLS, within-country fixed effects, Difference-GMM, and System-GMM with Windmeijer-corrected standard errors.

Findings

The negative instability-growth association survives the move from PWT 6.2 to PWT 11.0. In the extended heterogeneity analysis, cabinet changes reduce per-capita growth most strongly in low-income countries, have a smaller negative effect in middle-income economies, and show a small positive association in high-income countries.

Interpretation

The results support an institutional complementarity interpretation: weak state capacity, shallow financial markets, and weaker property-rights enforcement amplify the macroeconomic cost of political disruption. The thesis treats the income-group results as exploratory rather than definitive causal estimates because interaction-term instrumentation remains a limitation.

Honors Thesis in Economics, George Mason University. Advisor: Prof. Jason Dunick. PDF.

Abstract: This paper re-examines the relationship between political instability and economic growth using a global panel of 185 countries over 1960-2023. A near-reproduction of Aisen and Veiga (2013) confirms that the negative instability-growth association is robust across specifications. Extending the sample and allowing for income-group heterogeneity, political instability significantly reduces per-capita growth in low-income countries, has a smaller negative effect in middle-income economies, and shows a statistically positive association in high-income countries. The findings are consistent with an institutional complementarity channel in which weak state capacity amplifies the macroeconomic costs of political disruption.

A Recursive-Additive Closure for Chain-Weighted Superlative Growth Rates

Background

Chain-weighted superlative indexes provide meaningful growth rates but do not produce real levels that add up across components. This creates persistent problems for growth accounting and decomposition work that needs exact adding-up.

Data

The current paper applies the framework to BEA U.S. industry national accounts data. Related research work also tested recursive real value-added construction using Japanese national accounts data.

Methodology

The paper introduces a recursive-additive aggregation operator that treats official component superlative growth rates as primitives and constructs an exactly additive system of real levels over time.

Findings

Conventional chain-weighted real levels show large and persistent non-additivity, while the growth-rate distortion from recursive additivity is negligible and robust to base-year normalization.

Interpretation

The result reframes non-additivity as a missing accounting closure rather than an unavoidable limitation of superlative index theory.

Working paper under review at Journal of Economics. PDF.

Nonlinear Impacts of Particulate Matter on Solar PV Generation

Background

Solar output is often treated as a function of sunlight and weather, but particulate pollution can reduce generation through atmospheric scattering, absorption, and panel soiling. Chungcheongnam-do is a useful case because coal plants, industrial complexes, coastal inflow, and expanding solar capacity coexist in the same region.

Data

The study constructs a high-frequency spatiotemporal panel by merging hourly solar PV generation with PM10, PM2.5, and meteorological variables for Chungcheongnam-do.

Methodology

Hourly fixed-effects regressions with radiative and weather controls are estimated using Newey-West standard errors. The analysis also uses nonparametric exploration and wind-speed by 16-direction interaction models to test nonlinear and directional heterogeneity.

Findings

PM10 and PM2.5 both reduce PV generation, with PM2.5 showing the larger estimated effect. The pollution-PV relationship is nonlinear and potentially non-monotonic, and generation losses intensify under coastal-to-inland wind directions.

Interpretation

The results suggest that solar forecasting and grid operation should model air quality and meteorology jointly, especially in regions where renewable generation is exposed to industrial pollution and transport-pathway effects.

Journal of Environmental Policy and Administration, 33, 141-182. Co-first author with Moon Joon Kim. Article.

Abstract: We construct a high-resolution spatiotemporal panel for Chungcheongnam-do, Korea, by merging hourly solar PV output with PM10/PM2.5 and meteorological variables, and estimate the impact of air pollution on PV performance. Hourly fixed-effects regressions with radiative and weather controls show significant negative effects of both PM measures, with larger impacts for PM2.5. Nonparametric evidence suggests a nonlinear relationship between pollution and PV output, while wind-speed and direction interactions reveal stronger losses under coastal-to-inland inflow directions.

AI and Intangible Investments in Korea

Background

The project asks which firms adopt AI and whether intangible assets, firm scale, and complementary digital technologies help explain uneven AI diffusion across Korean firms.

Data

The analysis uses Statistics Korea's Survey of Business Activities from 2017 to 2023, covering annual firm-level records with employment, tangible and intangible assets, value added, industry classification, and adoption of AI, Big Data, IoT, Cloud, 3D printing, and robotics.

Methodology

Descriptive industry and firm-size comparisons are combined with an AI intensity measure based on complementary technologies and adoption purposes. The project uses growth accounting-style decomposition of labor productivity growth into real value-added and employment growth, and estimates dynamic labor productivity models using OLS, fixed effects, first differences, and System GMM. A binary probit model is also used to study AI adoption determinants, with marginal effects computed by the delta method.

Findings

AI adoption rose from 1.4% of sample firms in 2017 to 5.9% in 2023. AI firms are more common in services, ICT-related sectors, and large firms. AI adopters have about twice the average labor productivity of non-adopters, and adopters hold substantially larger intangible assets. Average labor productivity growth is higher for AI firms than non-AI firms, but the dynamic panel estimates do not provide a definitive causal AI effect on productivity growth.

Interpretation

The results frame AI adoption as a capability-based process: firms with scale, data infrastructure, software, R&D, and organizational capital are better positioned to adopt AI. The productivity evidence is consistent with the productivity J-curve idea: AI may require complementary intangible investment, digital capacity, and time before its effects become visible in measured productivity.

CompNet-Japan Workshop, Gakushuin University, Tokyo. Co-authored with Hak Kil Pyo, Keun Hee Rhee, and Hyuntae Kim.

Abstract: Using firm-level records from Statistics Korea's Survey of Business Activities, this project studies the rise of AI adoption in Korean firms and its relationship with intangible assets, complementary digital technologies, firm size, and labor productivity. The analysis combines adoption-rate comparisons, AI intensity construction, labor-productivity growth accounting, binary probit adoption models, and dynamic panel estimators including fixed effects, first differences, and System GMM. AI adoption is concentrated among larger, more intangible-capital-intensive, and ICT-connected firms; AI firms show higher average productivity levels and growth, but the estimated direct effect of AI on productivity growth is not definitive.

Chain-weighted National Accounts and Non-additivity

Contribution

Implemented recursive real value-added calculations for Japanese national accounts data and extended the same accounting logic to U.S. BEA industry data for the recursive-additive closure paper. Helped turn the accounting problem into a reproducible empirical workflow with robustness checks across base years.

Methods

Treated published component chain-growth rates as primitives, recursively accumulated component real levels, defined aggregates as the exact sum of components, and measured the implied deviation from official chain-weighted aggregate growth.

Output

Produced empirical workbooks and figures documenting non-additivity in chain-weighted levels and showing that recursive additivity restores exact accounting identities with negligible growth-rate distortion. The outputs supported both the working paper and Asia KLEMS presentation materials.
